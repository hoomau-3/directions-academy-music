How the Music Was Made

From three keyboards and a tiny black-and-white screen in 1991 to a finished song in a few days.

How the music was made

I can no longer sing, and even when I could, I was often off-key.

I began using computers to develop musical ideas in the early 1990s, when MIDI, the new Musical Instrument Digital Interface, let me play every instrument from a keyboard. I created a dozen songs, though recording them to analog was cumbersome; digital technology improved the process later, but it was still unwieldy.

At least my band members never argued, I was the entire band.

Today, AI fulfills that early dream by turning my musical sketches into fully produced songs. I’m grateful it arrived in my lifetime.

From an Idea to a Song, Step by Step

1% inspiration, 99% perspiration. Here is every step from idea to release. Tap a step to open it.

  1. Inspiration · the 1%
  2. 1Find the themeEvery song starts with one true thing worth saying.

    MeMy life, the classroom, the curriculum, class handouts, student autobiographies, discussions, interviews and emails, significant emotional events, lightbulb moments, brain-worm thoughts, articles, textbooks, other books

    This is the easiest part of the journey: taking a single idea and finding its emotional core.

    The human development curriculum I taught was a rich source. It already came with purpose and a hopeful outcome.

    My students were a deeper well still. Their hopes, dreams and tragedies, their stories about their own parents, and their determination not to repeat the same mistakes gave these songs their heart.

    Your turn: write down the one feeling or lesson your song must carry. Everything else serves it.

  3. 2Sketch the lyricsChoose the focus, then let the words come in their own time.

    MePen and paper, wherever ideas arrive: the shower, the desk, the bed, and sometimes by accident

    Choosing the focus turned out to be the hardest part, because there is so much to sift through.

    Then come the rhymes. I learned quickly that perfect rhyming is hardly necessary these days.

    I listened to many styles and genres and looked for patterns. I researched what young people in my audience actually listen to, and realized not everybody loves 60s classic rock. That was a lightbulb moment.

    So I listened to alternative R&B, alternative pop, hip hop, rap and more, and grew to appreciate them all. I learned more from my students than they ever did from me.

    Your turn: listen to the music your audience loves before you write for them.

  4. Perspiration · the 99%
  5. 3Edit with AI partnersAsk several AI partners how the words could reach further.

    Me, with Claude, Copilot and ChatGPTClaude, Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT

    Once the lyrical sketch was done, I ran it past several AI partners and asked how the words could reach more people.

    Each one sees something different. One tightens a line, another finds a stronger image, another asks whether a teenager would really say it that way.

    The final choice is always mine. The words have to still sound like me.

    Your turn: ask for suggestions, not replacements. Keep what is true, let go of the rest.

  6. 4Decide how it should soundDescribe the music before you make it: genre, beat, voice, instruments.

    MeGenre, style, beat and tempo, male or female voice, instruments

    This was a relatively easy step for me, after a lifetime of making music.

    I decided how the music should complement the words: the beat and timing, a male or female voice, the genre and style, and which instruments would work best.

    I wrote all of that down as a short description, which becomes the prompt for the next step.

    Your turn: you do not need to read music. You only need to describe what you hear in your head.

  7. 5Generate the song and voicesSuno turns the words and the description into a finished song.

    Suno, with me directingSuno (music and voices)

    Suno is an AI that can create a full song, music and voices together, from your words and a description.

    With almost no prompting it makes a lot of forgettable music. With carefully written prompts it can make something beautiful.

    Some songs came together in one to four takes. The toughest took a dozen, and I still was not fully satisfied. When one feels right, I mark it as the selected take and save the audio and the separate vocal track.

    Your turn: check the tool's terms. Releasing songs commercially usually needs a paid plan.

  8. 6Organize, plan and scheduleOne folder per song, one plan, and release dates spaced out.

    Claude, with meGoogle Drive, Google AI Plus, GitHub, Visual Studio Code, Cloudflare, spreadsheets

    Here is where Claude excels and became my most valuable partner.

    Every song gets its own folder: audio, lyrics, cover art, video and captions, all named the same way. One action plan spreadsheet tracks every task, and release dates are spaced out so each song gets its own moment.

    When I ran a research office, this kind of work kept a whole staff busy. I know that thought is scary for some folks. Do not fear this shift. Embrace it and see where it takes you.

    Your turn: set up your folders and your plan before song number two.

  9. 7Make the cover artA square cover with the title, the first thing people see.

    Claude, with meCanva, royalty-free photos, family photos

    Every song needs a square cover, 3000 by 3000 pixels, for the music stores.

    Claude painted ideas in Canva, added the song title and AiNa Lyfe, and I approved each one. For the tribute songs we used family photos.

    Your turn: keep the cover simple enough to read when it is the size of a postage stamp on a phone.

  10. 8Make the lyric videosPhotos, music and scrolling lyrics, with the words front and center.

    Claude, with meRoyalty-free photos, family photos, Claude's video scripts

    Once I had the finished audio, Claude built the videos.

    We spent hours together choosing royalty-free photos, placing them in order, and timing them to scrolling lyrics. One thing was certain: the words matter most, so in everything we made, the words are on display, front and center.

    Your turn: use only photos you have the right to use, and keep a list of where each one came from.

  11. 9Time the lyrics and captionsEvery line timed to the second, so listeners can sing along.

    Claude, with meCaption files for YouTube, synced lyrics for DistroKid

    Every line of the song is timed to the moment it is sung.

    Those timings become the captions on YouTube, so viewers can turn the words on or off, and the synced lyrics that scroll in Spotify and Apple Music.

    Claude found the timings from the vocal track. I listened and confirmed the tricky spots.

    Your turn: this step is tedious by hand. It is a perfect job for an AI partner.

  12. 10Build the websiteOne home for everything: songs, lyrics, stories and guides.

    Claude, with meGitHub, Visual Studio Code, Cloudflare

    Claude amazed me here. In a couple of hours he built a working website, and it became our home base.

    Every song has its own page with the video, the lyrics and the story behind it. There are album pages, a free lyric book to download, and guides for educators.

    I review every change before it goes live, and I press the button that publishes it.

    Your turn: everything you post anywhere should point back to one home.

  13. 11Upload for releaseSend the song to Spotify, Apple Music and the other stores.

    Claude, with meDistroKid

    DistroKid sends the song to Spotify, Apple Music and many other stores.

    The upload needs the audio, the cover, the cleaned-up lyrics, the songwriter credit, an honest answer about AI's part in the song, and a release date.

    Allow time. The stores need a week or more to process a new song, and longer if you want to pitch it.

    Your turn: pick a release date three or four weeks out.

  14. 12Prepare the releasePitch, schedule and prepare everything before release day.

    Claude, with meSpotify for Artists, YouTube Studio, Meta Business Suite, Instagram, TikTok Studio

    Once again, the administrative tasks are enormous and tedious.

    Pitch the song to Spotify's editors at least seven days before release. Schedule the YouTube video with its description, captions and playlist. Prepare lyric cards and short clips, and schedule the posts.

    Claude did most of this with me, one checklist at a time.

    Your turn: write a checklist once, then use it for every song.

  15. 13Release dayThe song goes live everywhere, and the word goes out.

    Claude, with meWebsite, YouTube, Spotify, Apple Music, Facebook, Instagram, Substack, TikTok, other platforms

    On release day the song goes live in the stores.

    The Spotify link is added to the song's page on the website, and the announcement goes out on Facebook, Instagram, Substack and TikTok, every post pointing back to the website.

    Your turn: celebrate. Then thank the people who listened.

  16. 14Keep goingListen to the response, learn, and start the next song.

    MeWebsite, YouTube comments, Substack

    A release is not the end.

    I read the comments, check what people are listening to, and improve the pages and guides. Teachers, nurses and counselors can use the songs and listening guides in their own work.

    Then I start the next song.

    Your turn: every song teaches you something for the next one.

Tools I tried and set aside: DaVinci Resolve, CapCut, Higgsfield, Kling AI, Runway, Metricool, Blotato, Pen.dev. Each is good at something; they just were not right for these songs.

1991 and Today

Here is what making a song looked like then, and what it looks like now.

1991Today

The computer

A Macintosh SE with a 9‑inch black-and-white screen.

A desktop with at least two monitors and lots of processing power, plus AI partners that never sleep.

The instruments

Three keyboards linked by a MIDI switch, just to get different voices and sound effects.

Every instrument and voice, described in a few words and played by Suno.

Making it human

In Performer, I drilled down to single notes and nudged the beat by a quarter or an eighth, building in mistakes so it would not sound mechanical.

The AI performs with natural feel. My job is to choose the take that feels right.

Recording

Only four tracks of analog tape. The tape stretched with use, and a stretched tape changes the pitch.

Digital files that sound the same on the thousandth play.

Time

Hours on every song, week after week.

A finished song in a few days.

Getting heard

Everything went through tightly controlled record and distribution companies. The odds were against almost every musician.

Streaming. A song can reach Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube on its own release day.

I welcome these new tools because I have been through the trenches, and it was not pretty.

Some of those 1991 songs survived the move from tape to MP3. With Suno Studio I have remixed them, and they will be shared here one day.

My creative partners

No song here was made alone. These are the partners who helped, and what each one does.

  • Suno turns my words and a short description into a finished song, music and voices together.
  • Claude is my everyday partner: planning, organizing, building this website, making the videos and timing every lyric.
  • Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT read my lyric sketches and suggest how the words could reach further.
  • Canva is where the cover art is painted and titled.

The words are always mine. The final choice is always mine.

About the author

I am David, a songwriter in my mid-seventies living in Hilo, Hawaiʻi.

For many years I directed the Research Corporation of the University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo, helping researchers win and manage their grants.

My passion project was teaching a human development course to college students, many of them on their way to becoming nurses.

Music has been with me the whole way, from MIDI in the early 1990s to AI today.

Why I write these songs

In all the time I taught human development to college kids, my students taught me as much as I taught them.

They shared their hopes, their dreams and their tragedies. They wrote about their own parents, and about their determination not to repeat the same mistakes.

Those stories stayed with me. These songs are my way of passing on what I learned: that a healthy mind grows in stages, and every stage can be met with care.

A little life lesson. No skip.
