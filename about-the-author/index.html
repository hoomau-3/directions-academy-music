About the Author

I am David, a songwriter in my mid-seventies living in Hilo, Hawaiʻi.

For many years I directed the Research Corporation of the University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo, helping researchers win and manage their grants.

My passion project was teaching a human development course to college students, many of them on their way to becoming nurses.

Music has been with me the whole way, from playing with my mother on the piano during "small-kid time" to several groups and bands to MIDI in the early 1990s, up to AI today.

Before the music

Before any of this, I spent eight years in medical research and the medical lab. I am a product of the Vietnam War era.

In the microbiology section of a dermatology research department, I worked on experiments to find solutions to help soldiers cope with the jungle: mosquitoes, sunburn, and jungle rot, a fungal infection spread by the same organism behind athlete's foot.

Later I left active duty, became an Army reservist, and went to work in San Francisco.

Into The Night

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, I commuted by train into San Francisco for work. Every morning I walked past people living in cardboard boxes, with rags wrapped around their feet. They were everywhere.

Every evening, when I got off work, the boxes were mostly empty. The people had slipped off into the night, I guess in fear that the police would try to move them into shelters. By the next morning the boxes were full again.

The problem was impossible to ignore, yet most people seemed determined not to see it. I was a musician, so I responded the only way I knew how. I wrote a song: "Into The Night."

My group, The Country Rebels, recorded it. We called them groups back then. "Band" made us think of the big bands of the 1940s, and most of us were born in the rebellious 1950s. Tony sang lead with his gravelly, powerful voice. George played rhythm guitar, Von played drums and, to everyone's surprise, performed a rap section he had never tried before. I played keyboards, guitar and bass, and we all sang backup.

The plan was simple. Get the song into the hands of the right people, and use any money it made to help the people living on those streets. We thought it was a good song. I still do.

I remember the day we recorded it. I didn't know it would be the last time we would ever get together. A war was starting in the Middle East. Desert Shield turned into Desert Storm, and I was called up as an Army reservist.

I was sent to Frankfurt, Germany, where I supervised the reservists in the lab and led organizational effectiveness sessions at the American hospital. After four or five months, I came home.

"Into The Night" now has its own home in Because of You, as my thank-you to The Country Rebels.

Where it began

When I came home, it was the early 1990s. Getting a group together had always been hard: schedules conflicted, rehearsals were complicated, and recording sessions were hard to coordinate. So I took up MIDI, a new technology that changed everything for me. For the first time, one person with a keyboard could play every instrument in the band.

My studio was a desk. On it sat a Macintosh SE with a 9‑inch black-and-white screen, three keyboards linked through a MIDI switch, and a four-track tape recorder.

Why three keyboards? Each one had its own voices and sound effects. To get a piano, a string section and a drum kit into one song, I switched between them, part by part. Remember, everything was new and expensive, so this setup was what I could afford.

The hours

I worked in a program called Performer. It let me drill down to a single note and move it.

A computer plays perfectly in time, and perfect time sounds mechanical. So I nudged notes off the beat by a quarter or an eighth, one at a time, to build in the small mistakes that make music sound human.

On that tiny screen, a single song could take hours upon hours, night after night.

Four tracks and a stretching tape

Recording was the hardest part. I had only four tracks of analog tape to hold everything, and then I mixed those four down to a two-track tape.

Tape wears. Every pass through the machine stretched it a little, and a stretched tape plays back slower. The voices and instruments sank lower in pitch.

To get a decent sound again, I had to speed up the playback, more or less depending on how far each tape had stretched.

Still, playing every part myself was far quicker than getting a group together. When I did get everyone in one room, getting us all on the same page took hours and hours. And there were no cloned voices to choose from back then.

The odds

I mailed my two-track tapes of "Into The Night" to record labels and producers, again and again. Nobody wanted it. Almost every time, it came back unopened and unheard.

There was no streaming, no YouTube, no way to reach listeners on your own. Record labels and distribution companies decided what the world would hear, and the process was stacked against the independent musician.

I grew frustrated quickly. So I stopped writing for them, and started writing for me. Everything I made at that desk was instrumental: orchestral pop, jazz, and exotic and native sounds.

Those songs waited on tape. In 2007 I finally copied them to MP3, and they waited some more.

Those MP3s still carry the hiss of the tape. The sound is muddled, and some songs sound artificially pitched. Listening now, I can hear how primitive home recording equipment was back then. What a difference today.

Full circle

After that, I put music aside for a very long time. It wasn't until August of this year that I even entertained the idea of making music again.

Then I looked again at a poster my students had made. In it, they are holding up cards thanking me for the class, and telling me how it changed their lives.

That poster told me I had one more job to do before I go. These songs are that job.

Today I can take an idea to a finished song in a few days. AI fulfills the dream I had at that desk in the early 1990s, and I welcome these new tools because I have been through the trenches, and it was not pretty.

Those early pieces have not been forgotten. I have remixed them in Suno Studio, and they are here now as an album called Awakenings. It was my first creative period, and it was my awakening.

Then and Today

Here is what making a song looked like then, and what it looks like now.

ThenToday

The computer

A Macintosh SE with a 9‑inch black-and-white screen.

A desktop with at least two monitors and lots of processing power, plus AI partners that never sleep.

The instruments

Three keyboards linked by a MIDI switch, just to get different voices and sound effects.

Every instrument and voice, described in a few words and played by Suno.

Making it human

In Performer, I drilled down to single notes and nudged the beat by a quarter or an eighth, building in mistakes so it would not sound mechanical.

The AI performs with natural feel. My job is to choose the take that feels right.

Recording

Only four tracks of analog tape. The tape stretched with use, and the music sank lower in pitch.

Digital files that sound the same on the thousandth play.

Time

Hours on every song, week after week.

A finished song in a few days.

Getting heard

Everything went through tightly controlled record and distribution companies. The odds were against almost every musician.

Streaming. A song can reach Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube on its own release day.

Why I write these songs

In all the time I taught human development to college kids, my students taught me as much as I taught them.

They shared their hopes, their dreams and their tragedies. They wrote about their own parents, and about their determination not to repeat the same mistakes.

Those stories stayed with me. These songs are my way of passing on what I learned: that a healthy mind grows in stages, and every stage can be met with care.
