The story behind the song

"Take Him Slow" is a song for my wife and partner, Eileen.

We often think the people who change our lives do it through dramatic moments. More often, they do it through thousands of ordinary acts of patience, kindness, and grace.

This song began with a phrase that kept circling through my head in the shower:

You don't know what you don't know.

It took about two weeks of showers before those words finally grew into a song.

As I've grown older and faced significant health challenges, I found myself wrestling with a fear that many people eventually face: what would happen to the person I love after I'm gone?

For years, I worried about Eileen.

But that worry led me back to the young man I was when we first married. I was so certain of everything. So confident. So convinced that I understood how life worked.

Age has a way of exposing those illusions.

Looking back, I can see how often I mistook confidence for wisdom and certainty for strength.

What I eventually realized was that I had the story backward.

Eileen was never the one who needed protecting.

She was the strong one all along.

This song is a tribute to a woman who saw through the bravado of a cocky young man and loved him anyway. Through patience, gentleness, and quiet persistence, she taught him to become better than he would have become on his own.

She showed me how to face aging not with resignation, but with grace. How to accept uncertainty without fear. How to measure strength not by control, but by endurance.

In the end, the song is really about a simple truth:

Sometimes the greatest gift one person can give another is to love them long enough to grow into who they were meant to be.

Eileen did that for me.

What the song is saying

"A man don't know what a man don't know"

The song begins with an old truth about learning.

When we're young, knowledge feels like certainty. As we grow older, wisdom often feels like the opposite. The more you learn, the more aware you become of how much remains unseen.

I was a cocky young man who thought he understood far more than he did. I had plans, opinions, and confidence to spare.

What I lacked was perspective.

It takes a good woman to take him slow.

"She finished the growing my mother left undone"

Every parent eventually reaches a point where their child stops listening.

A young man may leave home convinced he is fully grown, but life has a way of revealing how much work remains unfinished.

The line is not a criticism of my mother. Quite the opposite.

It is an acknowledgment that growing into a man is often a relay race. A mother carries the baton as far as she can. Then, if you're fortunate, someone else helps carry it farther.

Eileen did that for me.

Forty Years in White

One of my favorite images in the song is Eileen's long career in nursing.

She climbed the ladder one rung at a time, beginning as a Medical Assistant and eventually becoming a Professor of Nursing.

Her students loved her, and they still do.

Whenever I am admitted to a hospital, it is not unusual for a former student to recognize her name. More than once, those former students have become my nurses, and they have cared for me with extraordinary kindness.

A teacher's influence travels farther than the teacher ever sees.

Eileen's certainly has.

"Helpless at the Door"

The older I became, the more I worried about what would happen to Eileen after I was gone.

Looking back, it seems almost absurd.

Long before she ever met me, she had already endured hardships that would have broken many people. She faced them, survived them, and kept moving forward.

The evidence was right in front of me.

Her career.

Her character.

Her resilience.

So why was I afraid?

Because love sometimes confuses caring for someone with believing they need you to survive.

They are not the same thing.

The Lightbulb

Near the end of the song comes the realization that inspired it.

For years I imagined I was the one holding everything together.

I thought I was the one keeping the roof above us.

Then one day I saw it differently.

I wasn't the roof.

She was every beam.

That realization changed the song.

In truth, it changed much more than the song.

A Thank You

At its heart, Take Him Slow is not a love song in the usual sense.

It is a gratitude song.

A thank you to a woman who spent nearly fifty years teaching me lessons she never needed to speak aloud.

Lessons about patience.

About endurance.

About grace.

About growing older without becoming smaller.

And about the quiet power of loving someone long enough to help them become who they were meant to be.

Eileen, thank you for almost fifty years.
